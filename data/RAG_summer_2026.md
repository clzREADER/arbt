## Article Purpose

The article investigates titanium/silicon mono- and co-doped hydrogenated amorphous carbon (a-C:H) films deposited by mid-frequency magnetron sputtering. The primary research goal is to compare the effects of single-element doping (Ti or Si) versus co-doping (Ti + Si) on the composition, surface morphology, microstructure, mechanical properties, and tribological properties of the films. A key motivation is to determine whether the sp³/sp² carbon bond ratio can be regulated by a combination of Ti and Si dopants to achieve superior tribological performance, particularly ultra-low friction, in ambient air conditions. The study aims to clarify how co-doping elements jointly adjust the bonding configurations of carbon to produce films with tailored diamond-like or graphite-like qualities.

## Tribological Test Conditions

- **Tribometer:** Reciprocating ball-on-disc UMT-2MT (Center for Tribology, Inc., CA, USA)
- **Counterface material and geometry:** φ 3 mm steel ball
- **Substrate:** Films deposited on silicon substrates
- **Normal load:** 2 N
- **Sliding speeds:** 0.10 m s⁻¹ and 0.2 m s⁻¹
- **Stroke length:** 0.5 cm (reciprocating mode)
- **Temperature:** Room temperature
- **Relative humidity:** ~40% RH (ambient air)
- **Lubrication:** None (unlubricated dry sliding in ambient air)
- **Test duration:** Until film failure or steady state (Ti-C:H failed after ~250 s; others run to steady state)
- **Aging test:** Co-doped film also tested after 14 months of exposure to ambient air
- **Wear measurement:** 3D surface profilometry (wear rate in mm³ N⁻¹ m⁻¹)
- **Number of repeats:** Not explicitly stated for each condition

## Core Results

**Friction coefficients:**
- Ti-C:H film: Worn out quickly after approximately 250 s of sliding; very poor tribological performance
- TiSi-C:H (co-doped) film: Ultra-low steady-state friction coefficient μ < 0.01 (on the order of 0.01) at sliding speed 0.1 m s⁻¹ in ambient air with 40% RH — a superlow friction value
- Si-C:H film: Average friction coefficient of ~0.04 with comparatively large fluctuations in the friction curve
- After 14 months of ambient air exposure, the co-doped TiSi-C:H film still retained its superlow friction behavior at a higher sliding speed (0.2 m s⁻¹), though the initial run-in period was longer and transient friction was higher due to surface oxidation

**Wear rates:**
- TiSi-C:H: ~2.4 × 10⁻⁷ mm³ N⁻¹ m⁻¹ (extremely low)
- Si-C:H: ~2.8 × 10⁻⁷ mm³ N⁻¹ m⁻¹ (extremely low)
- Ti-C:H: not reported (film failed too quickly)

**Mechanisms and property correlations:**
- The sp³/sp² ratio is identified as the key structural parameter governing tribological performance:
  - Ti-C:H with extremely low sp³/sp² (0.28) → low hardness, polymeric-like carbon film, poor tribological performance, film wore out quickly
  - Si-C:H with high sp³/sp² (0.62) → more rigid carbon network, unfavorable for formation of carbon-rich transfer layer, resulting in higher friction coefficient (~0.04)
  - TiSi-C:H with intermediate sp³/sp² ratio (between 0.28 and 0.62) → optimal balance, favorable for forming carbon-rich transfer layers on the counterface, enabling superlow friction (μ < 0.01)

- The co-doped film's sp³/sp² ratio is jointly adjusted by the combination of Ti and Si: Ti promotes sp² bonding (reduces sp³ fraction by forming carbides), while Si promotes sp³ bonding (does not form π bonds). This cooperative effect enables the desired intermediate sp³/sp² ratio.

- Surface roughness: Not the critical factor — Si-C:H is smoother (0.49 nm) than TiSi-C:H (<0.70 nm) yet has higher friction.

- Hydrogen content: Not the critical factor — Si-C:H has highest bonded hydrogen content but higher friction.

- Water contact angle / surface energy: Not the critical factor — Si-C:H has highest contact angle (99°, lowest surface energy) but higher friction.

- SEM of counterface balls: Carbon-rich transfer layers were observed on the ball slid against TiSi-C:H film, while no effective transfer layer formed against Si-C:H (rigid network) or Ti-C:H (film failed).

- The superlow friction in ambient air is attributed primarily to the optimal sp³/sp² ratio achieved by Ti and Si co-doping, which facilitates the formation of a beneficial carbon-rich transfer layer.

## Article Purpose

The study aimed to investigate the tribological properties of Ti-doped diamond-like carbon (Ti-DLC) coatings under both dry friction and boundary lubrication conditions using poly-alpha-olefin (PAO) base oil. Two Ti-DLC coatings with distinctly different microstructures were prepared: one with low Ti content (4 at.%) where Ti atoms are dissolved in the amorphous carbon matrix, and another with high Ti content (27 at.%) where TiC nanocrystallites are embedded in the DLC matrix. The goal was to systematically compare their friction and wear behaviors, understand the role of microstructure on tribological performance, elucidate the lubrication mechanisms (transfer film formation, graphitization, oil film effects), and provide guidance for selecting DLC coatings under starved-oil and rich-oil conditions in practical engineering applications such as engine components.

## Experimental Procedure

### Deposition System
- **Method:** Hybrid ion beam deposition system consisting of a rectangular DC magnetron sputtering source with a Ti target (99.99% purity) and a linear anode-layer ion source (LIS).
- **Substrate:** Single crystal P(100) silicon substrates.
- **Substrate pre-cleaning:** Ultrasonically cleaned in acetone for 15 minutes, then etched in Ar plasma for 10 minutes to remove surface contaminations.
- **Precursor gases:** C₂H₂ (from ion source) at 10 sccm; Ar (from sputtering source) at 70 sccm.
- **LIS voltage:** 1500 V.
- **LIS current:** 0.2 A.
- **Substrate bias:** DC pulsed negative bias of −100 V with a frequency of 350 kHz, applied during both etching and deposition.
- **Target–substrate distance:** 20 cm (strictly controlled).
- **Deposition temperature:** Room temperature (RT).
- **Ti content control:** Adjusted by varying DC sputtering current.
- **Thickness control:** Kept at 520 ± 20 nm by changing deposition time.

- **Counterface material:** GCr15 steel ball.
- **Counterface geometry:** Φ 6 mm diameter, surface roughness Ra = 0.05 μm.
- **Normal load:** 5 N.
- **Sliding velocity:** 0.05 m·s⁻¹.
- **Total sliding distance:** 90 m.

**Dry friction:**

**Oil lubrication (PAO):**
- Both Ti4at.%-DLC and Ti27at.%-DLC exhibited low and stable friction with average friction coefficient in the range **0.09–0.13**, and no distinct run-in period was visible.

**Dry friction (after 3-min Ar⁺ etching at 2 keV):**
- **C 1s:** Four components — sp² C–C (284.2 eV), sp³ C–C (285.6 eV), C=O (288.2 eV), C–Ti (282.3 eV).

**Oil lubrication:**
- **C 1s:** Only three components — sp² C–C (284.5 eV), sp³ C–C (285.3 eV), C=O (288.6 eV); no Ti–C peak.

### Identified Mechanisms

**Dry friction:**
- **Ti4at.%-DLC:** Ti atoms react with C and O during sliding to form TiC and TiO hard phases, which aggregate into a transfer film that adheres to the steel ball. Combined with friction-induced graphitization producing easily sheared graphite-like lamellar structures, this results in the low, stable friction coefficient of 0.07. The transfer film also protects the underlying coating from further removal.
- **Ti27at.%-DLC:** The abundant TiC nanocrystallites (4–6 nm) in the DLC matrix act as hard abrasive particles at the sliding interface, causing severe abrasive wear, large friction fluctuations during the run-in period, and rapid coating failure. The higher compressive stress from the TiC-rich microstructure also promotes excessive graphitization, accelerating coating consumption.

**Oil lubrication (boundary):**
- PAO oil forms a hydrodynamic/boundary oil film (16 nm for Ti4at.%-DLC, 6 nm for Ti27at.%-DLC) that inhibits the formation of transfer film on the counterface and throws TiO and TiC hard wear particles out of the sliding interface, preventing them from accumulating as abrasive third bodies.
- For Ti27at.%-DLC, the absence of transfer of the graphitized carbon layer to the steel ball reduces coating material consumption, yielding the overall lowest wear rate of **1.12 × 10⁻¹⁶ m³·N⁻¹·m⁻¹**.
- For Ti4at.%-DLC, transfer of graphitized carbon still occurs to some degree, leading to slightly higher wear (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁶ m³·N⁻¹·m⁻¹) compared to Ti27at.%-DLC under oil lubrication.

## Article Purpose

This study investigates and compares the tribological behaviors of three thin-film coatings — diamond-like carbon (DLC), amorphous carbon (a-C), and titanium nitride (TiN) — deposited on biomedical-grade Ti6Al4V titanium alloy via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) methods. The research goals are: (1) to synthesize composite coatings with enhanced bonding strength by using a carbon ion implantation pretreatment layer for DLC and a-C films and a Ti transition layer for TiN film; (2) to characterize the microstructure, composition, thickness, hardness, surface roughness, and bonding strength of each coating; (3) to compare friction and wear performance under both destructive conditions (articulation against hard ZrO₂ ceramic ball) and normal conditions (articulation against UHMWPE polymer ball) in 25% bovine serum lubrication and dry sliding; (4) to identify dominant wear mechanisms for each coating/tribopair combination; and (5) to evaluate the potential applicability of these coatings for artificial cervical disk prostheses under different stress environments.

## Experimental Procedure

### Substrate Material and Preparation
- **Material:** Medical-grade Ti6Al4V titanium alloy.
- **Geometry:** Disk samples, thickness 5 mm, cut from a bar of diameter 30 mm.
- **Surface preparation:** Successively ground using 60 to 1200 mesh Al₂O₃ abrasive papers; final polishing with diamond paste to a mirror-like finish.
- **Initial surface roughness (Ra):** ~0.06 μm.
- **Cleaning:** Ethanol wash followed by hot-air drying.

### DLC Composite Coating
- **Pretreatment:** Carbon ion implantation.
  - **Equipment:** Ion implantation apparatus, Model LC-4 (Changsha, China).
  - **Dose:** 10¹⁶ ions/cm².
  - **Voltage:** 60 keV.
- **DLC deposition:**
  - **Equipment:** Plasma-based ion implantation system, DLZ-01 Multi-function ion implantation equipment (Harbin Institute of Technology, China).
  - **Deposition bias:** 20 kV.
  - **Pressure:** 1 Pa.
  - **Deposition time:** 10 h.
  - **Precursor gases:** C₂H₂ and H₂, flow ratio 20:1.

### a-C Composite Coating
- **Pretreatment:** Carbon ion implantation (same as DLC: 10¹⁶ ions/cm² at 60 keV, LC-4 apparatus).
- **a-C deposition:**
  - **Equipment:** rf-PECVD-2D chemical vapor deposition apparatus (Beijing Chuang Weina Technology Co. Ltd., China).
  - **Precursor gases:** CH₄ and H₂, flow ratio 1:3.
  - **RF power:** 300 W.
  - **Reaction pressure:** 4 Pa.
  - **Deposition temperature:** ~150 °C.
  - **Deposition time:** 90 min.

### TiN Composite Coating
- **Equipment:** Ion beam-assisted deposition system (State Key Laboratory of Tribology, Tsinghua University, China).
- **Ti transition layer:**
  - **Method:** Sputtering of Ti target with Ar⁺ beam.
  - **Duration:** 10 min.
- **TiN monolayer deposition:**
  - **Method:** Ti target sputtered by Ar⁺ beam from sputtering ion source for 220 min, simultaneously bombarded by N⁺ beam from assisting ion source.
  - **Sputtering ion source (Ar⁺):** Beam energy 2.5 keV, beam current 80 mA, ion flux 2.4 × 10¹⁴ ions/cm²·s.
  - **Assisting ion source (N⁺):** Beam energy 200 eV, beam current 30 mA, ion flux 4.8 × 10¹⁴ ions/cm²·s.

### Specimen Designation
- DLC (C ion implantation + DLC deposition), a-C (C ion implantation + a-C deposition), TiN (Ti transition layer + TiN deposition).

## Tribological Test Conditions

- **Tribometer:** Universal multifunctional tester (UMT), ball-on-disk reciprocating sliding mode.
- **Counterface materials and geometry:**
  - **Destructive test:** ZrO₂ (zirconia) ceramic ball, diameter 6.35 mm.
  - **Normal test:** UHMWPE (ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene) polymer ball, diameter 6.35 mm.
- **Normal load:** 3.0 N.
- **Initial maximum Hertzian contact stress:**
  - ~450 MPa for ZrO₂ counterface.
  - ~27 MPa for UHMWPE counterface.
- **Sliding speed:** 0.06 m/s.
- **Stroke/amplitude:** 3 mm.
- **Test duration:** 1 h (constant time to reach steady state).
- **Temperature:** 37 ± 1 °C (to simulate in-vivo physiological conditions).
- **Lubrication:**
  - 25% bovine serum (for tests with both ZrO₂ and UHMWPE balls).
  - Dry sliding (for UHMWPE ball tests only, to observe larger wear characteristics).
- **Replicates:** Each test condition repeated three times; fresh balls used for each test.

## Core Results

### Friction Coefficients (COF)

**With ZrO₂ ball in 25% bovine serum (destructive test):**
- TiN: 0.236 (highest)
- Untreated: 0.228
- a-C: 0.212

The article addresses the major limitations of aluminum alloys—low hardness (~1.74 GPa for the uncoated substrate) and poor wear resistance—that hinder their use in automotive moving parts (transmission bearings, tappets, gears, pistons). - **Below 100 °C**: Wear mechanism dominated by **surface polishing effects**. The Ti-DLC coating remains intact; wear grooves are not clearly formed or only very slight. - **At 150 °C and 50 N (vs. steel ball)**: The Ti-DLC coating is **completely removed**; wear applied to the substrate. Cracked coating remnants and wear debris are located at the track edges. This indicates partial transformation to a graphite-like structure, which may provide solid lubrication (explaining the still-moderate friction coefficient of 0.117 despite coating removal).
- **Tribo-chemical reactions**: Raman peaks in the range 225–700 cm⁻¹ and a broad peak at 650–750 cm⁻¹ on worn surfaces correspond to iron oxide phases (Fe₂O₃ and FeO). The formation of iron oxides is attributed to dissolved oxygen in the oil lubricant and tribo-chemical reactions between wear debris from the substrate/steel ball and the surrounding atmosphere.
- **At 150 °C and 20 N (vs. CrN-coated pin)**: Brittle fracture and localized delamination occur on the worn surface; however, the Ti-DLC coating continues to provide lubrication with a low friction coefficient. - Increasing Al concentration reduces residual stress (beneficial) and friction coefficient (up to an optimum), but also reduces hardness and increases wear rate (detrimental).

- Al at 4.03 at.% represents the best compromise: good hardness, low residual stress, lowest friction coefficient, and acceptable wear rate.
- At very high Al (24.18 at.%), structural degradation to coarse/granulose morphology, significant hardness loss, increased wear, and formation of Al-C carbide phases occur.

## Article Purpose

The article aims to meet the friction and corrosion resistance requirements of helicopter transmission bearings operating under harsh environments. The authors prepared Ti/TiN/Ti-DLC multilayer composite films on a 40Cr15Mo2VN high-nitrogen steel substrate using a non-equilibrium (closed-field unbalanced) magnetron sputtering technique. The primary research goals were: (1) to investigate the effects of varying the titanium target sputtering current (0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 A) on the film composition, structure, mechanical properties, and tribological properties; (2) to evaluate the tribological and corrosion resistance of the developed films under acidic conditions (acetic acid solution, pH = 3); and (3) to assess the protective performance of the DLC films against the metallic substrate under combined corrosion–wear conditions in both acetic acid solution (pH = 3) and aqueous solution.

## Experimental Procedure

### Substrate Material
- 40Cr15Mo2VN high-nitrogen steel (bearing material)
- Silicon wafers and 718 steel blocks also used as substrates for film preparation

### Deposition Method and Equipment
- Closed-field unbalanced magnetron sputtering device (CF-800, Teer, Birmingham, UK)
- Four magnetron sputtering targets: two carbon (graphite) targets, two titanium targets
- Bias: pulsed DC; target current: DC supplied
- Magnetic fields of adjacent magnetron targets are of opposite polarity (closed-field configuration)

### Deposition Process (4 steps)
1. **Argon ion etching**: Pump chamber to vacuum, introduce argon gas, adjust substrate bias for argon ion etching to remove surface contaminants
2. **Ti primer layer**: Adjust titanium target current, deposit Ti bonding layer
3. **TiN transition layer**: Adjust carbon and titanium target currents, introduce nitrogen gas, deposit titanium nitride transition layer
4. **Ti-DLC top layer**: Introduce methane (CH₄) gas, adjust titanium target current, maintain carbon target sputtering current at 5 A, deposit Ti-doped a-C:H:Ti films with varying Ti content

### Deposition Parameters (Table 1)

| Sample   | Ti Target Current (A) | Bias Voltage (V) | CH₄/Ar (sccm) | C Target Current (A) |
|----------|----------------------|-------------------|---------------|----------------------|
| Ti-0A    | 0                    | −200              | 40/30         | 5                    |
| Ti-0.5A  | 0.5                  | −200              | 40/30         | 5                    |
| Ti-1.0A  | 1.0                  | −200              | 40/30         | 5                    |
| Ti-1.5A  | 1.5                  | −200              | 40/30         | 5                    |
| Ti-2.0A  | 2.0                  | −200              | 40/30         | 5                    |

### Film Architecture and Thickness
- **Ti primer layer**: approximately 0.4 μm (columnar crystal structure)
- **TiN transition layer**: approximately 0.9 μm
- **Ti-DLC top layer**: approximately 1 μm
- Total multilayer thickness: approximately 2.3 μm

### Pre-deposition Cleaning
- Substrates ultrasonically cleaned in deionized water and acetone, then dried

### Surface and Cross-sectional Morphology (SEM)
- Cross-section: uniform structure, good density, no obvious defects
- Ti primer layer (~0.4 μm): typical columnar crystal structure
- TiN transition layer (~0.9 μm)
- Ti-DLC top layer (~1 μm)
- Surface: clustered particles present on film surface; particle density gradually decreases with increasing Ti current; surface is smoothest at Ti-2 A

### Raman Spectroscopy
- All films exhibit characteristic DLC features: D peak at approximately 1350 cm⁻¹ and G peak between 1500–1600 cm⁻¹
- I(D)/I(G) intensity ratio: minimum at Ti-0 A (undoped), maximum at Ti-2 A
- Interpretation: sp³-C/sp²-C ratio decreases with increasing Ti current (lower I(D)/I(G) indicates higher sp³ content)
- Ti doping promotes transition from sp³ to sp² hybridized carbon structure

### Mechanical Properties (Nanoindentation, load 5 mN, depth ≤ 1/10 film thickness, 5 points per sample)

| Ti Current | Nanohardness (GPa) | Elastic Modulus (GPa) |
|------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| Ti-0 A     | 16.33               | 139.99                 |

## Core Results

### Friction Coefficient (Dry Sliding, 20 N, 25% RH)
- High initial friction during running-in phase due to surface microbump particles
- Friction coefficient decreases and stabilizes after running-in period
- Friction coefficient gradually **decreases** with increasing Ti target current
- Ti-0 A: large fluctuation throughout friction period due to large surface particles
- **Ti-2 A: lowest friction coefficient ≈ 0.028**, shortest running-in distance ≈ 20 m

### Wear Volume and Wear Rate (Dry Sliding)
- **Ti-0 A (undoped)**: largest wear volume ≈ **1.85 × 10⁶ μm³**
- **Ti-2 A**: smallest wear volume ≈ **0.35 × 10⁶ μm³**
- Wear volume decreases with increasing Ti doping
- Wear mechanism: predominantly **furrow wear** (abrasive grooving)
- Wear track depth becomes shallower with increasing Ti current, shallowest at 2 A
- Wear spots on counterface balls show carbon film transfer; adhesion is slight at Ti-2 A

### Effect of Ti Current on Properties (Summary)
- Increasing Ti current (0 → 2 A):
  - sp³/sp² ratio **decreases** (graphitization increases)
  - Hardness **decreases** (16.33 → 11.25 GPa)
  - Elastic modulus **decreases** (139.99 → 115.65 GPa)
  - Bond strength **increases** (19.54 → 41.69 N)
  - Friction coefficient **decreases**
  - Wear volume **decreases**
  - Surface particles **decrease**, surface becomes smoother
- **Best overall performance**: Ti-2 A sample

### Corrosion–Wear Results (Ti-2A Film vs. Substrate)

#### Friction Behavior in Solutions
- Film friction coefficient is **lower than substrate** in both acetic acid solution and aqueous solution
- Film friction coefficient: decreases then stabilizes
- Substrate friction coefficient: increases then stabilizes
- Wear volume of film is **significantly smaller** than substrate in both solutions (good protective role)
- Wear volume in acetic acid solution is **higher** than in aqueous solution for both film and substrate (due to higher corrosiveness of acetic acid)
- Wear marks of film are shallower and narrower than substrate in both solutions

#### Open-Circuit Potential (OCP)
- OCP of film is **higher (more positive)** than substrate in both acetic acid solution (pH = 3) and aqueous solution → film has better corrosion resistance
- OCP in acidic solution is overall lower than in aqueous solution → more prone to corrosion in strongly corrosive media
- During friction: sharp drop in OCP at sliding onset due to surface state change; then stabilizes (dynamic equilibrium between removal and regeneration of passivation film)
- After friction ends: OCP shifts positively due to passivation film regeneration

The Ti-2 A film provides the best combination of tribological and corrosion properties: lowest friction coefficient (~0.028), lowest wear volume (~0.35 × 10⁶ μm³), highest bond strength (41.69 N), and significantly improved corrosion resistance over the uncoated substrate in both neutral aqueous and acidic (pH = 3) environments. The DLC film protects the substrate by reducing both mechanical wear and electrochemical corrosion, though dynamic wear conditions accelerate corrosion through continuous surface damage.
